# Overnight Mash Analysis: Red Bitter Astringency & Recipe Design

## The Problem

The beer had a noticeable **tannin-like astringency**. The mash was done overnight: mash-in at ~67C, left to cool unassisted to ~45C over approximately 8 hours.

## Theory: Special W + Overnight Mash = Astringency

**Verdict: Confirmed.** The astringency is almost certainly caused by the combination of a generous Special W charge and the extended low-temperature mash. Here is the detailed mechanism.

### What happens as the mash cools from 67C to 45C?

As the mash cools through various enzyme ranges, different processes activate sequentially:

**67C to 60C — Saccharification winding down:**
Beta-amylase (optimal 60-65C) and alpha-amylase (optimal 67-72C) continue working. By 60C, starch conversion is essentially complete. The wort becomes highly fermentable because beta-amylase has had extended time at its optimal range.

**60C to 55C — Protein rest zone:**
Proteinase enzymes (optimal 55-58C) become active, breaking down medium- and long-chain proteins into smaller peptides. Extended time here degrades the proteins responsible for body and foam stability. This is why overnight mashes can produce thinner-bodied beers.

**55C to 45C — Beta-glucanase / acid rest zone (the critical zone):**
- **Beta-glucanase** (optimal ~45C) breaks down beta-glucans, the gummy structural components of grain cell walls. In an already well-modified grain bill, this dismantles cell wall structures unnecessarily.
- **Peptidase** (optimal 45-53C) continues breaking proteins into very small peptides and amino acids.
- **Phytase** (optimal 35-45C) can lower mash pH over extended time.

### What is Weyermann Special W?

- **Type:** Drum-roasted caramel/crystal malt where the grain is stewed (saccharified inside the husk) and then kilned at higher temperatures
- **Color:** ~110-130 EBC (42-50 Lovibond) — deeply colored
- **Flavor:** Strong raisin, dark fruit, bready, toffee. A more complex, darker alternative to Caramunich III
- **Husk character:** The high kilning temperature significantly modifies husk polyphenols into forms that are **more easily extractable**. Contains higher polyphenol/tannin precursors than pale base malts, both from husk modification and the melanoidin-rich interior
- **Cell structure:** As a caramel malt, its interior is already saccharified during production — the cell structure is softer and more vulnerable to enzymatic breakdown

At 10.7% of the grain bill, this is an aggressive Special W charge. Most recipes use 3-8%.

### The Five Compounding Mechanisms

No single factor would likely cause a noticeable problem alone, but together they compound into perceptible astringency:

#### 1. Extended polyphenol extraction (primary factor)
Polyphenol extraction from grain husks is **time-dependent regardless of temperature**. Even at normal mash temperatures, the longer grain sits in water, the more polyphenols leach out. Eight hours is roughly 8-10x a normal mash duration. This alone significantly increases tannin levels.

#### 2. Beta-glucanase breaking down cell walls (significant contributor)
As the mash drops to 45-50C, beta-glucanase becomes highly active. This enzyme breaks down the beta-glucan "glue" holding cell walls together. In Special W, which has already had its cell structure stressed by high-temperature kilning, this enzymatic attack **exposes the interior polyphenol-bearing husk material** that would normally remain locked within intact cell structures. Beta-glucanase effectively "opens the gates" for tannin extraction from the heavily kilned husks.

#### 3. Extended protein rest degrades protective proteins
Proteins in wort can bind with polyphenols and prevent them from causing perceived astringency (this is the principle behind finings like gelatin). Extended time at 50-58C degrades these medium-chain proteins, removing a natural buffer against tannin perception. The beer ends up with both **more tannins extracted** and **fewer proteins to mask them**.

#### 4. pH drift during extended mashing
Over 8 hours, mash pH can shift. Phytase activity at lower temperatures produces phytic acid, which can lower pH. However, other buffering reactions can cause the pH to drift slightly upward as buffering compounds are depleted. If pH rises above 5.8, polyphenol extraction increases dramatically. Even modest increases from 5.3 to 5.6 measurably increase tannin extraction.

#### 5. Special W is the worst-case scenario for this
Compared to other specialty malts, Special W is uniquely problematic for overnight mashing because:
- Deep kilning creates more extractable polyphenol precursors than lighter caramel malts
- As a caramel malt, its interior is pre-saccharified during production — the cell structure is softer and more vulnerable to enzymatic breakdown
- Its melanoidin-rich character means it has more Maillard reaction products, some of which contribute to perceived harshness when combined with tannins

### Summary

The problem was **not the overnight mash itself** — it was combining an overnight mash with a heavily kilned, husk-rich specialty malt at a high percentage. Lighter specialty malts (e.g., Caramunich I or Carahell) would have been significantly more forgiving.

## Recipe: "Overnight Haze" — A Beer Designed for Overnight Mashing

### Design Philosophy

For an overnight mash (67C cooling to 45C over 8 hours), the ideal recipe should:
- **Minimize husk-derived polyphenols** — avoid heavily kilned specialty malts, favor huskless adjuncts
- **Benefit from extended beta-glucanase / protein rest activity** — wheat and oats benefit from these rests
- **Tolerate a thin, highly fermentable wort** — styles where dry/crisp is a feature
- **Embrace the enzyme activity** rather than fighting it

The perfect style is a **Hazy Wheat IPA / White IPA hybrid**:
- Wheat malt is **huskless** — dramatically reduces tannin/polyphenol risk
- Oats are **huskless** — same benefit, plus silky mouthfeel even after protein degradation
- Wheat and oats contain **high beta-glucan levels** that benefit from the beta-glucanase rest at 45C (improved lautering)
- The NEIPA/hazy style tolerates (even benefits from) the protein profile shifts
- Heavy late/dry hopping masks any minor process flaws

### Target Stats

| OG | ~1.058 |
| FG | ~1.010-1.012 |
| ABV | ~5.8-6.2% |

### Grain Bill (5.6 kg total, 65% huskless)

| Malt | Amount | % | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weyermann Pilsner | 2.5 kg | 44.6% | Light base malt, low polyphenol contribution |
| Weyermann Wheat Malt | 2.0 kg | 35.7% | **Huskless** — zero husk tannin risk. High beta-glucan benefits from the 45C rest. Provides protein haze |
| Viking Oat Malt | 0.8 kg | 14.3% | **Huskless** — beta-glucanase does useful work here. Adds silky mouthfeel that survives protein degradation |
| Weyermann Carahell | 0.3 kg | 5.4% | Very lightly kilned (~25 EBC). Minimal husk polyphenol risk. Adds a touch of honey sweetness |

**Rice hulls:** Add 200-300g for lautering insurance with 65% huskless grain.

Ferment at 20C.

### Overnight Mash Instructions

1. **Mash in at 67C.** The high wheat/oat content means you want good gelatinization and initial saccharification at this temperature.

2. **Insulate and walk away.** The mash will cool to ~45C over 8 hours. This is fine because:
   - Starch conversion completes within the first 1-2 hours as temp passes through 60-67C
   - The protein rest (55-60C) will break down some medium-chain proteins, but wheat and oat proteins are more resilient and the haze will persist
   - The beta-glucanase rest (45-50C) **actively helps** by breaking down the high beta-glucan content from wheat and oats, making lautering much easier

3. **Next morning:** Heat to 76C for mash-out (recommended with high wheat/oat content to improve lautering viscosity).

4. **Sparge gently** — keep sparge water below 76C and stop when runnings gravity drops below 1.010.

### Process Notes

- **Water chemistry:** Target a chloride-forward profile (150ppm Cl, 75ppm SO4) for soft mouthfeel. Keep mash pH at 5.2-5.3 — the lower end helps compensate for any pH drift during the overnight rest.
- **The overnight mash actively helps this recipe.** Wheat and oats traditionally need step mashes with protein rests and beta-glucanase rests. The overnight cool-down naturally provides these rests — what would normally require active temperature manipulation happens automatically.
- **No heavily kilned specialty malts.** The darkest grain is Carahell at ~25 EBC, compared to the Red Bitter's Special W at 110+ EBC. There are no deeply kilned husks for the enzymes to expose tannins from.
- **65% huskless grains** means beta-glucanase at 45C does useful work (breaking down beta-glucans) rather than harmful work (exposing tannin-bearing husk material).

### Mash Water Calculation

Per the standard formula (litres of water + kilograms of malt = litres at pre-boil):

If pre-boil volume is ~27L: mash water = 27 - 5.6 = **21.4L**

## Alternative Grain Bill: 50/25/25 Pilsner/Wheat/Flaked Wheat

An even safer overnight mash option:

| Malt | % | Husk? | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pilsner | 50% | Yes (light) | Lightest kilned barley malt, least modified husks |
| Wheat Malt | 25% | No | Huskless, provides protein for haze |
| Flaked Wheat | 25% | No | Huskless, pre-gelatinized, zero polyphenol source |

This is **equally or more safe** than the Overnight Haze recipe:

- **Flaked wheat is even safer than malted wheat** — it's pre-gelatinized, unmalted, and has zero husk material. There is literally nothing for polyphenols to come from.
- **50% total wheat** means only half the grist has husks at all, and those are the lightest Pilsner husks.
- The beta-glucanase rest at 45C **actively helps** break down flaked wheat's raw starches and beta-glucans, which is exactly what unmalted adjuncts need.
- Rice hulls (300g+) are essential with 50% huskless grain and no oats for lautering.

**Trade-off:** Flaked wheat contributes less protein diversity than malted wheat — more starch/body contribution, slightly less haze stability from protein-polyphenol complexes. Minor consideration for hazy styles, irrelevant for clean wheat beers.

## Pale Ale Malt vs Pilsner for Overnight Mashing

Pale Ale malt (e.g. Maris Otter) is **slightly worse** than Pilsner for overnight mashing, but not dramatically:

| Property | Pilsner | Pale Ale (Maris Otter) |
|---|---|---|
| Kilning temp | ~80-85C | ~95-105C |
| Husk polyphenol state | Least modified, least extractable | Slightly more modified |
| Maillard products | Minimal | More (biscuity/bready character) |
| Diastatic power | Higher | Lower (irrelevant — conversion finishes in 1-2h anyway) |
| Tannin risk (relative) | 1x (baseline) | ~1.1-1.15x |

The difference is **small** — maybe 10-15% more polyphenol extraction potential from Pale Ale. Nothing like the 5-10x difference between either of them and Special W. You could use Maris Otter in an overnight mash and be fine, especially if the rest of the grist is huskless.

### Malt safety spectrum for overnight mashing

```
SAFE                                                          DANGEROUS
|----|----|----|----|----|----|----|----|----|----|----|----|
Flaked   Wheat   Pilsner  Pale Ale  Vienna  Munich  Crystal  Carafa
Wheat    Malt                                       60-120   Special W
Flaked                                              Caramunich Chocolate
Oats                                                III      Roasted
                                                             Barley
```

**Rule of thumb:** The darker the kilning, the worse it is for overnight mashing. Huskless adjuncts are always safe. Keep heavily kilned malts (<80 EBC) below 5% in an overnight mash, and avoid anything above 100 EBC entirely.

---

## Key Takeaway

The overnight mash is not inherently problematic — the issue is **what grains you put in it**. Deeply kilned, husk-heavy specialty malts (Special W, Carafa, dark crystal malts) are the worst candidates. Huskless grains (wheat, oats, rye) are the best candidates, because the low-temperature enzyme rests that happen overnight actually benefit them. Design the grain bill around the process, and the overnight mash becomes an advantage.
